Minjerribah · North Stradbroke Island · Quandamooka Country

Three townships, no bridge, and a day that runs on the tide.

Minjerribah sits in Moreton Bay, about thirty kilometres east of Brisbane. Everything that is not already on the island arrives by water. This is the island as the two twins describe it, for someone who has never been.

Copernicus Sentinel-2, 13 July 2026 · sources

Where it is

A sand island off Brisbane, with three places people live.

Dunwich, also called Goompi, faces the bay and is where the boats land. Amity Point, also called Pulan, is on the northern corner. Point Lookout, also called Mooloomba, is on the headland at the top of the ocean side, above the North Gorge walk. Those are the names the twins publish, written the way the first build writes them: Dunwich (Goompi), Amity Point (Pulan), Point Lookout (Mooloomba).

There is no bridge. The first build's introduction says it in one line: "Everything that is not already there arrives on a barge."

That is the island as the Sentinel-2 satellite saw it on 13 July 2026, shaded with real elevation. What you can pick out at 17 metres a pixel: the surf beach running the whole eastern side, the sand banks of Moreton Bay to the west, the perched lakes inland, and the pale scar of the old sand mine. The first build draws its ground from a height map of 908 by 1314 cells, each 32 metres square, about 1.2 million cells, built in under a second when the island loads.

Dunwich (Goompi)

Where the boats land.

Both island landing points are here: the Junner Street ferry terminal and the One Mile jetty. The first build reads its tide from the Dunwich standard port, and the camera bookmark F1 flies to the township.

Amity Point (Pulan)

The northern corner.

The first build reads the tide here as a station of its own, and its on-screen introduction stands on the Amity Point shoreline, on the houses, the road and the rock wall, to explain the erosion levers. The coast module that would model the erosion itself is designed and not written.

Point Lookout (Mooloomba)

The headland.

"Streets of houses on a headland above a beach" is how the first build's README describes the view from 420 metres up. Whales sit off the headland in their season, drawn in daylight and not at night, and the North Gorge walk is the fourth camera bookmark.

Everything arrives by water

Three boat services, two operators, two landing points.

The second build keeps the boats in a crossings pack: a data file of records, each carrying its source. The records come from the two operators' published timetables and a photographed departure sign, supplied on 19 August 2026. All three services run from Cleveland on the mainland, and the pack calls the crossing "the throttle on every other system in this world", because there is no bridge.

Read against the island clock, those timetables give a person a way off the island from 05:15 to 20:45 on any day of the week, and a car from 07:00 to 17:30. That gap is why the second build refuses to add the two together. Its crossings pack states the rule: "Passenger and vehicle crossings are never one figure. You can almost always get off the island; you cannot always get your car off, and the second question is the useful one."

SeaLink calls its vehicle timetable a base timetable and adds sailings that appear only on its booking page, so any vehicle count from the pack is a floor. Two vessels work the barge run in that base timetable, Sea Breeze and Minjerribah; the second shares its name with the island, and the pack notes that nothing may read one as the other.

On the second jetty, the pack says: "For a foot passenger this is real redundancy against Junner Street. For a vehicle there is none."

The tide

"More of this island runs on the tide than on the clock."

That line is printed on the first build's Today board, the noticeboard the island opens as. The board leads with the tide at the Dunwich (Goompi) standard port. A standard port is the place a tide table is published for; other spots are worked out from it with a lag and a ratio, so high water at Amity Point is not high water at the Gorge. Beside the port the board reads four stations: Amity Point, the Gorge, Main Beach and One Mile.

The rule the island drives by is on the same board: "Main Beach and Flinders Beach are shut to vehicles for two hours either side of high water." The first build's transport pack works that through to the day: two closed windows, each about four hours, sliding roughly fifty minutes later each day, with beach traffic bunching against the edges of each window as a beach opens. The pack's record of the permit conditions adds that a four-wheel drive and a vehicle access permit are needed to reach the beach camping areas on both.

The barge timetable, the sand flats at Amity Point and whether you can walk the low shelf at the Gorge all follow the same water, and the first build's tide system lists them as the reason it exists. Its model is a harmonic one: seven constituents, meaning seven regular swings of known period added together. The second build ships zero constituents, because no published table for the Dunwich port has been obtained, and its ferry page marks the water level "contested or unknown" with a date rather than draw a curve.

Why here

Why this island carries the model.

An island is a closed system you can still walk across. Everything that arrives, arrives by water. Everything that fails, fails visibly and at once: a storm, a grid outage and a supply chain cut all show up the same way, as the barge not running and the shelves not filling. That makes Minjerribah a place where a whole-of-system model can be held in one head and still be about something real.

It is also consequential beyond itself. The sand under it holds a freshwater lens that has supplied the mainland since 1996 at about twenty megalitres a day, up to sixty per cent of the Redlands mainland supply, against about 1.6 megalitres a day used on the island. Twenty megalitres is eight Olympic pools, every day, one every three hours around the clock. Any model of this ground is already a model of somebody else's tap.

And the ground itself is the material the whole programme is made of. Not rock to be blasted but saturated sand that wants to collapse, sitting in a lens that is the heat sink, the water supply and the shielding at once. What holds true here decides what can be built here.

The frame it sits inside

A determination, and an Act.

Two Federal Court consent determinations of 4 July 2011 recognise Quandamooka native title rights and interests. The North Stradbroke Island Protection and Sustainability Act 2011 set the terms sand mining ended under. Anything proposed on this ground is read against both before it is read against engineering.

The scale

About 2,200 people, three towns, one crossing.

Small enough that a model can carry every household, every road and every arrival. Large enough that the answers are not toys.
