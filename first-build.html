Inside the first build · Minjerribah Living Twin

A place you can fly, click and read.

The first twin is a browser game engine island with 0 parts on this site's list. This page walks it the way a person meets it: the arrival screens, the noticeboard, the bar across the top, the cameras, the boards, the council in tray, and the measured list of what works and what does not.

Arriving

You arrive at an acknowledgement, then three doors.

On a first visit the build opens on an acknowledgement of Quandamooka Country, over the island at night. Beneath it sits a short list headed "What this project will not do". The fact of the build, stated there and in its documents, is that cultural material does not enter: no language words, story, art, cultural practice or sacred places, from any source or contributor. A line under the list opens where the wording comes from. Then a Continue button, a Skip link, and four dashes for the four arrival screens.

The arrival screens end at three doors. Two open onto the island itself. The third opens How it works: seven short screens that explain the machine with the island still in view, the camera moving onto whatever each screen describes. Press ? at any time to see the arrival screens again, and Y for How it works.

The simulated clock opens at 5:20am against a 5:39am sunrise, so the island behind those doors is sky and silhouette. Rather than leave you to conclude something is broken, a strip says the sun is five degrees under the horizon, says when sunrise is, and offers to move the clock to twenty minutes after it, the first moment there is light on the ground. That jump is written to the record like any other, and the same strip offers the way back.

Today on Minjerribah

The island opens as a noticeboard.

After the first visit, this board is what the island opens as, and T brings it back once dismissed. Each tile carries a chip, Real or A simulation, so you can tell at a glance which numbers are worked out from a published record and which the twin made up to keep the island living.

In the capture above the date reads Wednesday 2 September 2026, 11:10pm, and the season line reads "Warming. Queensland school term." The tide tile shows 0.08 m and falling, with high water 1.94 m at 5:10am and readings at four stations: Amity Point 0.19 m, the Gorge 0.39 m, Main Beach 0.42 m, One Mile 0.07 m. The tile explains the difference: the tide is worked out at the Dunwich (Goompi) standard port as a seven-constituent harmonic model, meaning seven regular waves of different size and timing added together, with a size ratio and a delay for each place, so high water at Amity Point is not high water at the Gorge.

The light tile gives sunrise 6:00am, sunset 5:33pm, 11 hours 33 minutes of daylight and a moon 51 per cent lit and waning, with a note that the moon is an approximation good for how much light there will be rather than to the minute. The crossing tile puts two questions and says only one of them fails: in the board's words, "Getting your vehicle across is the one that books out." At 11:10pm both directions read "no more today", first tomorrow 7:00am to the mainland and 6:00am over to the island.

The conditions tile is the one marked A SIMULATION. Air and wind read "not known", because the clock is live and no real reading is in hand; the twin runs weather of its own so the island keeps living, and refuses to show that as though it were today. The tile ends: "Do not plan a day on this screen." A footer line lists what is real on the screen: the date, the season, the school calendar, the tide, the sun and moon, the published timetable, the events with their organiser and source, and the published opening hours.

The bar

The bar across the top says what kind of time you are looking at.

Along the top runs the heads-up bar: air, wind, swell, tide, UV, fire danger, crossing conditions, and the clock with its mode. The mode chip changes the moment the kind of time changes, and there are three kinds.

  • Simulated is the default. The clock starts at a moment written in the record and runs forward from it, seeded, so the same island happens again on anybody's machine.
  • Live puts the island's clock at the real Queensland moment. It is an anchor, not a feed: the real date and time is taken once, stamped into the record, and nothing reaches the network. Island time is AEST all year.
  • Scrub is a parked clock looking at a moment the island is not at. The sun, the moon, the tide at every station and the calendar can be worked out exactly for any minute, so they follow you. Everything that has to be lived through, the weather, the queue at the ramp, which shops are open, stays at the island's present and is tagged HELD. Forward of the present is a projection, and the bar says so.

The time keys, from the build's keyboard file:

SpacePause and unpause. From a parked clock, come back to the island's present and play.
1 2 3 4Speed: 1x, 3x, 12x, 60x. Choosing a speed is how you leave live.

Going live and coming back to now are permanent buttons on the bar rather than keys, on purpose: a person who does not know they are looking at a projection will not press a key to find out.

Cameras

Five ways of looking, and four places to jump to.

The camera bar along the bottom of the screen lists the five modes: Planner, Street, Follow, Drone and Cinematic. Tab steps to the next one and Shift + Tab to the previous. Planner is the view from above; Street puts you on foot; Follow tracks a thing you have selected; Drone flies, with Space to climb; Cinematic runs shots, and C moves to the next one.

Four bookmarks sit beside the modes: F1 Dunwich, F2 Amity Point, F3 Point Lookout, F4 North Gorge. Shift with a function key saves your current view over that slot, and Alt + 1 to 4 reach the same four places without the function keys. Home frames the whole island.

Photo mode, P, hides the whole interface and carries a time slider of its own. That slider is the same scrub as the bar's, through the same clock, and it declares itself; nothing in photo mode advances the island.

The civic chain

You are not the mayor.

The build's own explainer says it in three words: "You are not the mayor." The civic board carries sixty-two researched levers, a lever being one thing a council or another body could decide to do, with what it costs to build, what it costs to run, how long it takes and who decides it. That last field is the point.

Every one of the nineteen belongs to Redland City Council, so the seat the twin puts you in is a council one: Division 2, which covers Cleveland on the mainland and North Stradbroke Island together, so the island's representative also answers to a mainland suburb with far more voters. Of the rest, the Queensland Government is named on twenty-five levers, QYAC on fourteen, the joint management arrangement over the national park on ten, SeaLink on six and Minjerribah Camping on four. The barge is a commercial service with a commercial timetable, no ballot and no published resident quota, so on a long weekend your lever is asking.

What you do instead is the game. Pick a lever and read who decides it and what it claims it will do. Open a file, and the in tray gives you:

  1. Five commissionable studies, each with a real week count and a real price, which you pay for.
  2. A lodge-it-anyway path, which comes back as an information request twelve weeks later.
  3. A consultation, and a submission you can make properly.
  4. A petition, which the board describes as a list of names unless a consultation came back in favour.

Where a decision belongs to a body the twin will not model or guess at, it stops and says so. You can leave the matter pending, or record your own assumption and carry the label. Anything QYAC decides sits in that category: the twin models the shape of the process, waits, and states that it does not model the decision. Press G for the board.

Measured 9 August 2026

What works today, and what does not.

Both lists are from the build's README, measured on 9 August 2026 by playing the whole thing end to end and running a simulated year with no picture. The numbers are the README's numbers.

Works

  • The island: 271 km² of land, high point 233 m, a heightfield (a grid of height values) of 908 by 1314 cells at 32 m, about 1.2 million cells, built in under a second. At 32 m a cell is a little over one and a half cricket pitches on a side.
  • 55 systems and layers registering with zero console errors, and nothing thrown after driving every panel and pressing every key.
  • A simulated year completes with zero runtime errors, and two runs of the same seed agree over 1,440 ticks.
  • 13 boards and 15 render layers, a render layer being one kind of thing drawn: terrain, ocean, sky, trees, buildings, roads, people, vehicles, fauna, weather, the works below the sand, the data views, the selection and the finishing pass.
  • About 2,300 people drawn at a township in four draw calls (one draw call is one instruction to the graphics chip), vehicles on the road graph, whales off the headland in daylight, koalas in the trees.
  • The civic chain, end to end, as described above.
  • The seams carry: a koala hit reaches the rescue call, the notification bar, the chronicle and the conservation groups' mood; a cancelled barge rolls vehicles and gets written up. Eighteen of twenty-two lived-event channels fire over a simulated year.

Does not

  • The tick is over budget: 6.23 ms against 4 ms. A tick is one step of the simulation, ten simulated minutes; six thousandths of a second is a small fraction of a blink, and the budget of four exists so the whole picture can hold sixty frames a second on a laptop's built-in graphics. Draw calls are comfortable at 115 to 179 against a budget of 900, so the cost is in the simulation, not the drawing.
  • Close range is hazed and washed out. Street level in a township has almost no contrast, and the bay-side water renders a saturated green rather than turquoise.
  • Five modules named in the design are not written: fire, coastal erosion, groundwater, shared presentation read models, and the stewardship board. They are listed as planned and reported at start-up rather than requested and failed.
  • Several read models describe the instant rather than the day. A read model is the summary a part publishes for the boards to show. A probe taken at 5:20am reports zeros for a ferry that carried thousands of people in the previous day, because the daily counters reset at midnight. The ferry is the clearest case.
  • Sixteen per cent of vehicle route requests fail, every one because the geography pack does not carry the street: 328,801 failures out of 2,182,279 requests in a simulated year, about six thousand requests a simulated day. The twin counts the failure rather than drawing a car on a road that is not there, which is why the roads look quiet.
  • The story layer is thin against its brief. Over a simulated year, 12 threads opened and 35 beats fired against 514 cast failures, and most days are quiet.

The engineering handover behind these lists, with the measured numbers and the known traps, is docs/STATE-OF-PLAY.md in the repository. Next: every part of both builds, or open this one now.
