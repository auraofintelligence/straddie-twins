What a twin is

A model that can say where each number came from.

The phrase digital twin is used for many things. On this site it has one working meaning, set by the first build's own introduction: a model of Minjerribah built from what the island has published about itself, with a clock, that runs whether or not you touch it, that gets things wrong, and whose use is that every number can say where it came from.

Copernicus Sentinel-2, 13 July 2026 · sources

The definition

Built from the public record, with a clock on it.

A digital twin is a working copy of a real place kept in software. It is not a map and not a photograph: it has a clock, and things in it happen in order, so you can ask what the island looks like at four o'clock on a Friday and get an answer worked out for that minute. The first build's introduction gives its twin four properties, and the second build holds itself to the same four.

  1. It is built from what the island has published about itself. The ferry timetable, the road network, the business directory, the tide, the council's own list of decisions. Every record names where it came from.
  2. It has a clock and runs whether or not you touch it. In the first build: "Two thousand-odd residents get up, go to work, walk the dog, put the bins out on bin night and go to bed. The tide turns. The barge sails, or the wind gets up and it does not."
  3. It gets things wrong. The introduction says so, then says why that is bearable: "The useful thing about it is not that it is right. It is that every number in it can tell you where it came from and how much weight it will hold."
  4. Every number carries its record. Click anything in the first build and the inspector fills with what it is, where the record came from and how much weight it holds. On the second build's ferry page every value points at a record on disk, and one of its checking tools fails the page if a number points at a record that does not exist.

Three registers

Real, modelled, proposed: you should be able to tell them apart on sight.

A register is the kind of claim a value is. Everything on the first build's screen is one of three, and its introduction defines them like this.

Real

It exists, somebody published it, and the record names where.

The vehicle barge timetable. The road network. Which beaches are gazetted for vehicles, meaning declared open to them in an official government notice, and the tide window that shuts them.

Modelled

The twin worked it out. Nobody measured it.

How many people are on the island this afternoon, what a shop took today, how many koalas are in a patch of forest. In the introduction's words, "a modelled number is an argument, not an observation," and the panel that shows it says what it rests on.

Proposed

Somebody has suggested it and it does not exist.

It stays marked proposed in every panel. The biggest example is a proposed works below the sand: not built, not approved, not funded, not sited and not consented, and every screen about it opens by saying so.

One function decides which register a record is in, and the checker that guards the data runs the same function, so nothing can be a proposal in one place and a fact in another. The introduction adds that the badge is not yet drawn the same way in every panel: the classification is shared, the drawing is not. Two more markers sit beside the three, and there is a fourth state with no badge at all: anything below the confidence threshold is not shown. The build calls that held, and treats held as normal.

Unconfirmed

A real thing nobody has been able to check lately.

Thirty-one businesses carried the marker when the introduction was written, with counts as at 10 August 2026. The reasoning is the build's: a Proposed badge on a shop that is probably open would be a worse falsehood than the one the badge exists to prevent.

Contributed

Supplied by a contributor and not yet verified by a second source.

One is in the running build: a seafood business at Amity Point that several directories still list as trading. A resident said it had closed, the correction was recorded on 6 May 2026, and the record is kept closed with the correction attached so a later pass does not re-add it from a stale listing.

Not known

No fresh source can answer, so the page says so and gives a date.

The second build's marker. Where it has nothing published to stand on, its ferry page prints "contested or unknown" and the date it last looked, "as at 19 August 2026". The first build's Today board does the same for air and wind when the clock is live and no reading is in hand: the words "not known", not a number.

The second build names its registers known, modelled, proposed and contributed. Known is its word for real, and its front page tags every value KNOWN or MODELLED.

How time works

The bar across the top says what kind of time you are looking at.

A tick is one step of the clock. The first build's tick is ten simulated minutes; the second build's is one island minute, 1,440 to a day, which is 24 hours times 60. In the first build the clock has four states, and the bar names the one in force before you have to ask.

Simulated

The default. The clock starts at a moment written down in the record and runs forward from it.

It is seeded, meaning every random choice grows from one starting number, so the same island happens again on anybody's machine.

Live

The island's clock is at the real Queensland moment.

The introduction calls it "an anchor, not a feed": the real date and time it started from is taken once and stamped into the record, and nothing about it reaches the network. Island time is AEST, ten hours ahead of Coordinated Universal Time, all year; the introduction notes that Queensland has observed no daylight saving since a referendum on it was defeated in the summer of 1991-92.

Scrub

You park the clock and look at a moment the island is not at.

The sun, the moon, the tide at every station and the calendar can be worked out exactly for any minute, so they follow you. The weather next Tuesday, the queue at the ramp and which shops are open cannot be conjured from a timestamp, so they stay at the island's present, and every cell holding one of those numbers is tagged HELD.

Projection

Forward of the island's present. "Nothing there is a record of anything."

You can turn a projection into history by running the ticks the island skipped, and the twin tells you how many that is before it starts. Back before the present is not automatically history either: if the simulation did not run that Tuesday, you are looking at a real sun and a real tide over an island that was not there, and the twin draws only the stretches it has lived through.

The second build keeps two times on every fact: when it was true of the island, and when the twin believed it. Live, history and scenario are then three positions of one cursor over the same store, and a correction adds a new belief instead of overwriting the old one. Its ferry page states the minute its answers were worked out for and says of itself: "It is not a live position report and no boat here is being tracked."

What a twin is not

Not a game about a made-up place, and not the island.

It is not a game about a made-up place.

In the first build's words: "Every business, road, beach, headland, club, agency and ferry service in it is a real one, taken from a public record, with the record named in the data. Nothing was invented to fill a gap." Where a fact could not be found, the gap is still there and is usually visible. The second build holds the same line harder: no published tide table for the Dunwich port has been obtained, so its tide carries zero constituents and its page shows a dated blank rather than a plausible curve.

It is not the island.

It is a model of the island, built from what the island has published about itself, and it gets things wrong. The residents in the first build are generated by the simulation, about two thousand of them, given homes, jobs and relationships by the population system. Neither build sends anything anywhere. The first writes three files a person can read and send on, a day of the island, a noticeboard notice and a rescue draft, and states the rule itself: "The twin never posts anything anywhere by itself."

The first build also carries one refusal as a fact of the build: cultural material does not enter it, from any source or any contributor. The acknowledgement both builds open with is on the about page, with the licence.

Two builds, one idea

The same definition, taken from two ends.

The first build is a living world you can fly around; the second started again from the instruments up and today answers one question from records it can show. The next page says what the first taught and what the second does differently.
