Two builds, kept apart

The first build ran before it could measure. The second measures first.

Two digital twins of the same island exist because the first one, built fast and all at once, kept a record of everything it got wrong. The second was started from that record. Both still run.

Same island, two orders of work

One was built as a world. The other was built as a set of instruments.

A digital twin is a working model of a real place, made from what the place has published about itself, with a clock on it so the day can run. Minjerribah has two, and they were built in opposite orders.

First build

Minjerribah Living Twin

Built by Luke Catalyst Nathan Hayes with Claude (Opus 5) working as a swarm: many copies of the model, each building a separate part at the same time. Its record runs from a full measurement on 9 August 2026 to the hand-over of its lessons on 18 August 2026.

It runs in a browser game engine, Babylon.js 9.20: the kind of software that draws a three-dimensional world many times a second, copied into the repository so the page fetches nothing. Everything was attempted at once: the ground, the tide, the weather, koalas, whales, residents, the ferry, a council in tray, the island's own written chronicle.

Second build

second-island

Started clean on 18 August 2026. It began with instruments: the checking tools that judge every later part, each one proven by breaking a copy of the build on purpose and watching the tool notice.

Its README describes the starting point: "There is no island yet: there is a sun, a tide that says it does not know its own height, and the instruments that will judge everything after them." Since then it has gained the boat and bus timetables and the ferry page that reads them.

  • The island. 271 km² of land with a high point of 233 m, built as a heightfield (a grid of height values, one per cell, 908 by 1314 cells at 32 m each, about 1.2 million cells) in under a second. Flown to Amity Point at 900 m the shape reads as the real place: the spit, the jetty, the two streets, the bay banks.
  • A simulated year completes with zero runtime errors. It did not until that pass: a text value where a number belonged killed the story engine about ninety simulated days in, every time.
  • Determinism holds. Deterministic means the same starting number, called a seed, gives the same island on anybody's machine. Two runs of the same seed agree over 1,440 ticks, a tick being one step of the simulation, ten simulated minutes.
  • 13 boards and 15 render layers. The bar with air, wind, swell, tide, UV, fire danger and crossing conditions; an island log with search; 22 views that paint data over the ground; a civic board; an inspector that fills when you click anything; a map; a calendar; a siting bench; the chronicle; the proposed works below the sand; settings; a generated soundscape with a mixer.
  • People, vehicles and wildlife on screen. About 2,300 people at a township in four draw calls (a draw call is one instruction to the graphics chip; fewer is cheaper), vehicles on the road graph, whales off the headland in daylight and not at night, koalas in the trees.
  • The civic chain works end to end. Pick a lever, read who decides it, open a file, commission a study with a real week count and a real price, or lodge the thing anyway and get an information request back twelve weeks later.
  • The seams between parts carry. A koala hit on the road reaches the wildlife rescue call, the notification bar, the chronicle and the conservation groups' mood. A cancelled barge rolls vehicles, moves the commuters and gets written up. Eighteen of twenty-two lived-event channels fire over a simulated year; two of eleven did before that pass.
  • A camera with planner, street, follow, drone and cinematic modes, bookmarks, labels, letterbox bars and a photo mode.

The same README lists what does not work, with numbers. Inside the first build carries that list in full.

The failure catalogue

What the first build's own record says went wrong.

The second build's founding document ends with a failure catalogue: nineteen rows, each with what happened in the first build, what it cost, and the rule that now prevents it. Read as plain facts about the first build, the rows that matter most are these.

  1. Passenger and vehicle ferries were merged into one figure. On an island with no bridge the useful question is whether your car can get off. With the two crossings added together, that question could not be answered.
  2. Rules lived in prose rather than in code. A cultural rule was written down, agreed, and then broken inside the same repository by a later pass that had read it: five deleted season names were still in use as keys in the drama pack, in twenty-eight places.
  3. The world opened empty at tick zero with the camera sixteen kilometres up. For eight days the owner did not see a car.
  4. Average frame rate was the metric. A frame is one drawn picture; a hitch is a frame that takes far too long. Averaging hid a 1.16 second hitch for a week.
  5. It invented where real data existed. 1,922 buildings were placed by rule beside real building outlines that were never used, and a simulated sky ran beside a free real weather feed. The catalogue counts 1,059 real outlines; the second build later found that figure covered a box over Moreton Bay, and that 601 of the outlines sit on this island.
  6. Every record carried one time. A record needs two: when a thing was true of the island, and when the model learned it. With one, every board that touched time carried a bug about looking forward.
  7. Its checking tools were never validated. Three rounds of verdicts were given against a broken picture, and the interface was judged from screenshots of the three-dimensional canvas, which the interface is not drawn on. Those verdicts contained nothing.
  8. It was built by subsystem, not by island event. Forty-six clean systems with dead seams between them, so a koala death reached nobody. The 9 August 2026 pass repaired several of those seams; see the list above.
  9. The playthrough was scheduled last and was cut off by session limits three rounds running. The crash on simulated day ninety was found a week late.
  10. Seventy-nine thousand lines of code were written before the first commit, so nothing could be wound back.
  11. No personal-data screen stood at the door. Six named individuals and a mobile number sat one commit from public.
  12. Findings died in transcripts. A bug that had been precisely diagnosed shipped anyway, and the owner hit it himself.
  13. The data pipeline grew lane by lane and was never drawn whole, until its owner had to say it was an unknown.
  14. A name was coined for one of Luke's own designs and quoted back to him as his.

None of this is a verdict on the first build. It is the first build's own working, carried into the second so that each row has a rule against it. The first build keeps running as the reference.

Since 18 August 2026

What the second build does differently.

Every rule is a runnable check, or says it is not.

Its contract opens with one line: "A rule that is not in the gate does not exist." The gate is a program that reads every file in the repository and refuses the build when a rule is broken. Every rule in the contract is either a named check in the gate, or carries the words NOT YET CHECKED and a reason. There is no third state.

Two times on every record.

Every fact carries when it became true of the island, when it stopped, when the twin learned it, and when the twin moved on from it. This is called bitemporal: two timelines on one record. A correction closes the old belief and opens a new one; nothing is overwritten. Live, history and scenario are three positions of a cursor over the same store.

No invention where real data exists.

The contract's words: "Where real data exists and is licensed, invention is a bug." No published table of tide constituents (the regular waves a tide is worked out from) for the Dunwich standard port has been obtained, so the tide ships zero of them and the page reads "contested or unknown" with the date, rather than a number.

The two crossings are kept apart.

The rule the build states about itself: "Passenger and vehicle crossings are never one figure." The timetable pack gives every sailing a field that reads vehicles or passengers with no third value, and offers no field where the two could be added. A gate rule checks it, proved by three planted faults.

Instruments proven by watching them fail.

A self-test breaks a copy of the build 33 ways and runs every instrument twice, healthy and faulted. A tool counts as proven only when it passes on the healthy copy, fails on the broken one, and names what broke. The matrix generated on 20 August 2026 reads 33 of 33 rows green across 21 instrument columns. One row is inverted on purpose: the screenshot tool is required to miss a changed label, which records that a picture of the canvas cannot see the interface.

The worst frame, not the average.

The frame-time tool reports the median, the 95th and 99th percentile and the single worst frame, and does not report the average, because reporting it invites someone to quote it. A planted stall of 40 ms every sixtieth frame was caught at frame 58.

A noticeboard that outlives the session.

Every finding is posted to a board inside the repository, one note per file. The rule: "A finding that lives only in a transcript is treated as never made."

Two repositories

Kept apart on purpose.

The second build's README states it: "The two repositories are deliberately separate and must stay that way." Work in one does not write into the other. The first island is read from the second as a reference and a test oracle, a known answer to compare against, and is not edited from there.

The separation is checked, not assumed. The first build serves on port 4271 and the second on 4281, and a gate rule in the second build fails it if either of the first build's ports is written down in its files. Data, constants and gate rules were copied across exactly: the cultural pack, for one, is byte for byte the same file in both repositories. Prose was not copied. The second build's contract says doctrine is rewritten for this build in this build's voice, citing the first as ancestry.

What each one is for, today:

  • First A living world to fly around, click and interrogate, with the record of what it gets wrong kept beside it.
  • Second A set of proven instruments, a record store with two times, and one everyday question answered from the printed timetables.
