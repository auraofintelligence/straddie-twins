Where the numbers come from

Every number carries its record, and a gap stays a gap.

Both twins are built from what the island has published about itself. This page is how a fact gets in, what it carries with it, what is shown and what is held back, and what each build does when it does not know.

Copernicus Sentinel-2, 13 July 2026 · sources

The first build

Four ways it can come to know something.

The first build's own introduction names four routes for a fact, and says which one does nearly all the work today. A pack, in both builds, is a data file of records: one subject per file, every record naming where it came from.

  1. Committed packs. Twelve of them in the README's count, about 1.9 megabytes, holding around fourteen hundred records with about 1,100 source fields and 760 source addresses between them. Most records carry a confidence of high, medium or low. This is where almost everything on screen comes from.
  2. Documents with page citations. A tool reads a named public document, pulls the lines that mention the island, and writes candidates carrying the page number and the sentence, word for word. It never writes a pack. A person reads the candidates and decides what is true, and a candidate nobody has read cannot become data. Two Redland City Council documents are registered, and no record had been promoted through this route when the introduction was written.
  3. Contributions from islanders. One is in the running build: a seafood business at Amity Point that several directories still list as trading. A resident said it had closed, the correction was recorded on 6 May 2026, and the record is kept, closed, with the correction attached, so a later pass does not re-add it from a stale listing. There is no form and no portal; it arrived in conversation and somebody typed it in.
  4. Synced feeds, with a freshness date. A sync is an offline step, run on purpose by a person, that reads a real source and writes a stamped, committed feed. The running twin still fetches nothing. Every synced record carries when it was synced, from what source, and what the source said about its own currency. Four feeds exist (weather, wildlife rescue, the noticeboard, the events engine); the running twin reads only the weather feed, and that feed goes stale after a day by its own rule.

Trading hours, and the threshold

Three words for an opening time, and a line below which nothing is shown.

The business pack grades every set of trading hours with one of three words, and the difference is the difference between a simulation that can tell you something about the island and one that can only tell you about itself.

Published

The business published them itself.

On its own site or page.

Listed

A council, a tourism body or an island directory published them.

Real and citable, one step further from the kitchen.

Estimate

Nobody published them.

A pattern for the simulation to run on, never shown as a fact about a real street.

Below a confidence threshold a record is not shown at all. The build calls that held, and treats held as a normal state: an unreviewed record is invisible, not caveated. Thirty-one businesses carried the marker unconfirmed, meaning a real thing nobody has been able to check lately, when the introduction was written with counts as at 10 August 2026.

Two other rules hold at the door. Locations are coarsened at intake, before anything is committed, never filtered at display time: wildlife rescue coordinates are rounded to about 110 metres and their minutes to the hour, and holiday lettings to 250 metres. The build's own words for why: "a runtime filter is one bug away from publishing the thing it was hiding." And no contact detail and no residential address enters the repository through a contribution, under any consent, from anyone.

The wanted list

What the first build does not know, counted out of its own packs.

The one device in the first build that looks like a game and is not: a visible list of what the twin does not know, counted from the packs as they stand. Trading hours nobody published. Premises with no coordinates, so they are in the pack but not on the ground. Businesses nobody could confirm are still trading. A cafe where two published sources give different street addresses, so its coordinates are left empty until a local says which one is right. The reward for filling a gap is that the gap is filled, with your name on the record.

The second build

A receipt for every fetch, and an inbox that is sealed.

The second build fetches a source only through a connector, a small tool run on purpose from the terminal. Every fetch writes a receipt beside the raw file: the address requested, the time in island time, the status the server answered, the size in bytes, a hash (a fingerprint of the exact bytes, so a copy can be checked), how long it took, and the licence as read. A failed fetch writes nothing, and the tool's own message is: do not type in a timetable in its place.

What a connector fetches lands in an inbox that is sealed: the folder is kept out of the repository's history, and one of the build's checks proves that by asking the version-control system itself, rather than trusting a setting. Five connectors exist, for OpenStreetMap, TransLink, past weather, the 2021 census and the Atlas of Living Australia. Only the bus pack has been committed from them.

Every record must point into a sources file, by id, never by free text. Each source entry carries its title, publisher, the exact request, the date it was retrieved, the licence text as it was read and the date it was read, and the attribution wording owed.

An absence, as a record

The tide table that was not obtained.

No published table of harmonic constituents for the Dunwich standard port has been obtained, so the tide pack carries zero of them and a record whose payload is the absence: the state "not obtained", the count zero, the source, the date. The ferry page reads that record and prints "contested or unknown" with the date rather than a curve.

The five packs

  • crossings: the boat timetables, transcribed and sealed.
  • transport-gtfs: the island bus feed, derived with its receipt.
  • tide: zero constituents and the absence record.
  • _sources: every source by id, with its licence.
  • lore: the acknowledgement wording, byte for byte the first build's file.

Open questions and an open decision

What the crossings pack says it does not know.

The crossings pack carries its open questions as records, each with the question, what is known and the possibilities, at confidence unknown. None was answered when this site was made.

  • Whether the vehicle barge is booked or turn up and queue.
  • Which extra vehicle sailings SeaLink adds beyond the printed base timetable; they appear only on its booking page.
  • Whether today is a public holiday: nobody in the build holds a public holiday calendar, and four Gold Cats Flyer departures state that they stop on one.
  • What changes in the school holidays: a different timetable, and no source in the build carries it.
  • How long the walk-on crossing takes: neither passenger operator publishes it.
  • Which mainland berth the Flyer uses, and how many berths Junner Street has.
  • Why two of fourteen mainland departures differ between the operators' two published sources.

One decision is open and it is the owner's, not the build's: whether to commit data that carries the Open Database Licence's share-alike condition, which OpenStreetMap data does. Until it is decided, the OpenStreetMap, weather, census and ecology packs stay in the inbox and a gate rule blocks them from the committed data. The build's licence questions document sets out the options and the questions that only the owner can answer. The bus pack is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution, so it did not wait.
